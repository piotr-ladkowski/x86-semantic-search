How search works

You describe what you want in your own words. doc/x86 finds the pages whose meaning is closest to your question, even when they do not use your exact words.

The idea: a map of meanings

Imagine a huge map where every sentence gets a pin. Sentences that mean similar things land close together, even if they use different words: “count the set bits”, “population count” and “hamming weight” end up in the same neighbourhood, while “add two numbers” lands somewhere else entirely.

Nobody wrote a rule saying those phrases are related. A small AI language model (it is called BGE-small, and it runs on this site’s own server) learned it by reading a great deal of text. To give a sentence its pin, the model reads it and answers with a list of 384 numbers: its coordinates. The real map has 384 directions instead of two, which is impossible to picture, but the idea is identical: nearby coordinates mean similar meaning. Such a list of numbers is called an embedding.

Step 1: pin every page, ahead of time

Each page gets several pins, not just one:

  • one for its summary and explanation, and
  • one for every question people might ask that the page’s author wrote down. For example, POPCNT carries “count the number of set bits” and “hamming weight”.

Why so many? A short question lands closer to another short question than to a whole paragraph. All of this happens once, when the site is built, and the result is stored. Step 2: pin your question, then find the neighbours

When you search, the same model turns your question into its own 384 numbers: a new pin. We measure how close that pin is to every stored pin. We really do check all of them; there are few enough that the comparison takes a fraction of a millisecond, and most of the time goes into reading your question. Each page is scored by its single closest pin, and pages are listed from closest to farthest.

“Closeness” is the angle between the two lists of numbers (the maths name is cosine similarity): do they point the same way?

Two small nudges for exact names

Meaning-based search is good at ideas and surprisingly bad at exact names, so there is a little plain keyword help on top:

  • If you type a name exactly (popcnt, LEA, xor), that page goes straight to the top. Alternative spellings of the same instruction (such as SAL for SHL) lead to the same page once it is written.
  • If a name appears inside a longer sentence (“what does lea do”), that page gets a small bonus. It is kept small on purpose, because names like AND, OR and CALL are also ordinary English words.

Where it can go wrong

  • It always shows its best guess. There is no “nothing found” cut-off yet, so a weak match can still appear. If the top result looks unrelated, the instruction you want probably is not written yet ({{ documented }} of {{ total }} planned instruction pages exist) or is out of scope: this site covers x86-64 user-space integer instructions, not SIMD/AVX, floating point or operating-system instructions.
  • Close scores are a tie. Scores within a few hundredths of each other do not mean the first result is better than the second. The model also leans on shared everyday words such as “number” as much as on meaning, so an unrelated page can briefly outscore the right one. Treat the top few results as candidates, not as one answer.
  • The model is small and reads English only. It captures meaning roughly, and unusual wording can confuse it. Rephrase your question, or try a mnemonic.
  • Finding a page is not the same as the page being right. Pages are written with AI assistance and checked against the Intel manual (the SDM); those marked draft have not been reviewed by a human yet. For anything that matters, confirm in the SDM, or open a page’s evidence to see which sentence of which document each statement rests on.
  • No outside AI service is involved. The language model runs on this site’s own server.
